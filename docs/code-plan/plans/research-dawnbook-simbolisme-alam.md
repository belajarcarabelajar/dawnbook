## 1. Method

- Base text: the owner's own study notes on the topic (13 modules, a local Markdown file outside this repository; path in the book test). Chapter authors read their line range there; these notes do not copy it.
- Every literary or factual claim the chapters keep was checked by four read-only researchers with TinyFish `search` and `fetch_content` (2026-11-05). Each item below gives the verified fact in paraphrase and the source row (section 9). Items marked CORRECTED replace what the owner's notes say; chapters state the corrected fact directly, with no mention of the old wording.
- Quotes: English and other foreign wording is paraphrased in Indonesian in the chapters. Short phrases quoted here (under 15 words) are for checking only.

## 2. Chapters 01 to 03 (items A1 to A9)

- A1 (CORRECTED). John Ruskin coined "pathetic fallacy" in Modern Painters, volume III (part IV), chapter XII "Of the Pathetic Fallacy"; the volume's preface is dated January 1856 and Merriam-Webster dates first use to 1856. He calls the fallacy a sign of a weak or morbid mind, yet grants it power when the feeling is genuine; his examples include Coleridge, Pope, Kingsley, Keats and Tennyson, so the critique is not aimed only at Romantic poets. Rows S1, S2.
- A2 (CORRECTED). Ruskin's classes (section 9 of that chapter): people who feel little and so see truly (he does not count them as poets); second-order poets who feel strongly, think weakly and see untruly; first-order poets who feel strongly, think strongly and see truly (he names Dante); and those overpowered by something greater than themselves, the condition he links to prophetic inspiration. Keats and Tennyson are his second-order examples. Row S1.
- A3 (CONFIRMED). "Pathetic" goes back to Greek pathetikos (subject to feeling), from paskho (to suffer); "fallacy" comes from Latin fallacia (deception), from fallere (to deceive). Rows S3, S4.
- A4 (CONFIRMED). The Poetry Foundation glossary defines the pathetic fallacy as giving human feelings to inanimate things, related to personification and anthropomorphism, with the emphasis on the link between the poet's emotional state and what the poet sees. Merriam-Webster's example is "cruel sea". Chapters may say personification lends human traits to any thing as a figure of speech, while the pathetic fallacy projects a mood onto nature; present it as a difference of emphasis, not two rigid categories. Rows S5, S2.
- A5 (CORRECTED). Macbeth, act 2, scene 4 (Ross and an Old Man, the morning after Duncan's murder): the day stays dark though the clock says day; the Old Man recalls that on the Tuesday before, a falcon was killed by a mousing owl; Duncan's horses turned wild and broke out of their stalls, and the Old Man says they are reported to have eaten each other, which Ross confirms he saw. Row S6.
- A6 (CORRECTED). The Lion King (1994): rain falls in the finale, after lightning sets the Pride Lands on fire and Scar is overthrown, and puts the fire out. No checked source shows rain at Mufasa's death; chapters must not claim it. Rows S7, S8.
- A7 (CONFIRMED). T.S. Eliot, The Waste Land (1922), part I "The Burial of the Dead", opens by calling April the cruellest month: spring rain stirs memory and desire in dull roots, while winter kept people warm under forgetful snow. The Poetry Foundation notes the line inverts Chaucer's restorative April. Rows S9, S10.
- A8 (CORRECTED, hedge). "Show, don't tell" is often credited to Chekhov. In a May 1886 letter to his brother Alexander he advised catching small details in nature writing, such as a glint of broken bottle glass on a mill dam, to suggest moonlight; the short line about the moon and broken glass is a later condensation. Write "sering dikaitkan dengan Chekhov" or "berangkat dari nasihat Chekhov dalam surat 1886". Row S11.
- A9 (CORRECTED). Northrop Frye, Anatomy of Criticism (1957), pairs four narrative mythoi with the seasons: comedy with spring, romance with summer, tragedy with autumn, irony and satire with winter. R. D. Denham's guide adds the matching life stages (youth, maturity, old age, death). Present the season-life mapping as Frye's cyclical scheme, a reading tradition, not a rule of literature. Row S12.

## 3. Chapters 04 to 06 (items B1 to B12)

- B1 (CONFIRMED, hedge). Plato, Republic book VII (514a-517c): a prisoner freed from the cave is pained and dazzled by the light, needs time to get used to it, and sees the sun itself last. That the sun stands for the Form of the Good is the standard reading of the allegory; write it as a reading ("biasa dibaca sebagai"). Row S48.
- B2 (CONFIRMED). Louis XIV chose the sun as his personal emblem early in his reign, linked to Apollo, and is known as the Sun King (Roi Soleil). Row S49.
- B3 (CONFIRMED, nuance). Ovid, Metamorphoses book VIII: Daedalus warns Icarus to fly neither too low nor too high; drawn by desire for the sky, Icarus soars higher, the sun softens the wax holding his feathers, and he falls into the sea that bears his name. Row S50.
- B4 (CORRECTED). Albert Camus, L'Étranger (1942): on the beach the heat and the glare disorient Meursault; the sun flashes off the Arab's knife blade like a sword in his eyes, and Meursault shoots him with a revolver; at his trial he says he killed because of the sun. The Arab is never named. Do not say the sun "drives him to murder without reason" as fact; it is the novel's own framing. Rows S51, S52.
- B5 (CONFIRMED, hedge). In Jung's work on alchemical symbolism (Mysterium Coniunctionis, Collected Works vol. 14), Sol stands for consciousness and Luna for the unconscious. Write "dalam simbolisme alkimia yang dibaca Jung", not "dalam psikologi Jung" in general. Do not claim the sun is masculine and the moon feminine as a fact of literature; at most say many traditions paired them that way. Row S53.
- B6 (CORRECTED). The Moon's phase cycle (new moon to new moon) lasts about 29,5 days; the orbit itself takes about 27,3 days. The notes' "28 hari" is wrong. Row S54.
- B7 (CONFIRMED). "Lunatic" comes through Anglo-French from Late Latin lunaticus, from luna (moon): originally a person whose disorder was thought to follow the moon's phases. Row S55.
- B8 (CORRECTED). The fixed rule that a werewolf changes at the full moon is largely a 20th-century film convention; older legends mention the moon only now and then. Write it as a popular image, not ancient lore. Row S56.
- B9 (CONFIRMED). Mary Shelley, Frankenstein (1818): Victor, at work in his laboratory as the moon rises over the sea, looks up and sees the creature at the window by moonlight. Bram Stoker, Dracula (1897): in his journal for mid-May, Jonathan Harker sees three women in the moonlight in his room at the castle. Use these two scenes, not a generic claim that Gothic climaxes fall at the full moon. Rows S57, S58.
- B10 (CORRECTED). The notes' "Wordsworth or Coleridge use the moon as a companion of solitude" is not supported as written. Usable instead: in The Rime of the Ancient Mariner, part IV, the moving moon climbs the sky while the becalmed mariner is alone among the dead crew. Drop the "companion" framing. Row S45.
- B11 (CONFIRMED, hedge). In Greek myth the Styx is one of the rivers around the realm of the dead, across which the dead are ferried by Charon. Write "salah satu sungai yang mengelilingi dunia orang mati". Row S59.
- B12 (CONFIRMED). The Old Man and the Sea: Santiago has gone 84 days without a fish; the boy Manolin has been sent to another boat, so Santiago rows out alone into the Gulf Stream on day 85. Owner chapter is 12; chapter 06 mentions the solitude in one clause. Row S37.


## 4. Chapters 07 to 10 (items C1 to C13)

- C1 (CONFIRMED). Dante, Inferno canto I: midway through the journey of life he finds himself in a dark wood (selva oscura) because the straight path was lost; heartened by a hill whose slopes are lit by the sun, he begins to climb, and three beasts drive him back. Rows S13, S14.
- C2 (CONFIRMED). Tolkien: Treebeard (Fangorn) is the oldest Ent and guards Fangorn Forest, in The Lord of the Rings, The Two Towers, chapter "Treebeard". Mirkwood belongs to The Hobbit, chapter 8 "Flies and Spiders", where Thorin's company goes astray among the endless trees. Rows S15, S16.
- C3 (CORRECTED). King Lear: in act 2, scene 4 Goneril and Regan each insist he dismiss his knights; he rushes out into the storm and they shut the castle doors behind him. Act 3, scene 2 opens with the direction that the storm continues, Lear and the Fool enter, and Lear rages at the storm and calls his daughters pernicious. Do not write "dipadang/heath" as the text's word; write "di tengah badai di luar kastel". Rows S17, S18.
- C4 (CONFIRMED). Nietzsche, Thus Spoke Zarathustra, prologue section 1: at thirty Zarathustra left his home for the mountains, enjoyed his solitude for ten years, then decided to go down to people to share his wisdom. Row S19.
- C5 (CORRECTED). "Lembah kekelaman" is Mazmur 23:4 in the Indonesian TB ("Sekalipun aku berjalan dalam lembah kekelaman, aku tidak takut bahaya"). Use "lembah kekelaman (Mazmur 23:4)". Row S20.
- C6 (CORRECTED). Bunyan, The Pilgrim's Progress (1678): Christian passes through the Valley of Humiliation (where he fights Apollyon), then the Valley of the Shadow of Death, later reaches the Delectable Mountains after Doubting Castle, and at the end the Celestial City. There are no "Celestial Mountains". Row S21.
- C7 (CONFIRMED, with limits). Hamlet, act 4, scene 5: Ophelia hands out rosemary "for remembrance" and pansies "for thoughts" (both glossed in the text, Laertes calls it thoughts and remembrance fitted), rue "herb of grace" worn "with a difference", plus fennel, columbines and a daisy with no meaning stated; the violets withered when her father died. Meanings for fennel, columbine, daisy and violet are editorial tradition only; chapters name the two glossed ones and may add rue, nothing more as fact. Rows S22, S31.
- C8 (CONFIRMED). Mazmur 103:15-16 (TB): a person's days are like grass, he blooms like a flower of the field, and when the wind passes over it, it is gone. This is the source of the notes' unattributed "flower of the field" sentiment; cite it as the Psalm. Row S23.
- C9 (CONFIRMED, nuance). Atamian and colleagues (Science, 2016): young sunflowers track the sun from east to west and turn back east at night; mature plants stop moving with their flower heads facing east. So the notes' "always faces the sun" holds only for young plants. DOI 10.1126/science.aaf9793. Row S24.
- C10 (CONFIRMED). The lotus grows in murky water and blooms above the surface; in Buddhist and Hindu art it is a sacred symbol of purity, awakening and transformation. Row S25.
- C11 (CONFIRMED). Carpe diem closes Horace, Odes 1.11 (to Leuconoe): pluck the day, trusting the next as little as possible. Memento mori means "remember that you must die". Rows S26, S27.
- C12 (CONFIRMED, nuance). The language of flowers (floriography) peaked as a fashion in the Victorian era, among the new middle class in Britain, France and the United States; the practice is older and much of the British tradition came from French flower books. Do not say the Victorians invented it. Row S28.
- C13 (CORRECTED). The House-Tree-Person drawing test is read by some clinicians as a window on self-perception, but its reliability and validity have little support and scoring is largely subjective; a 2023 meta-analysis found that drawn roots were the strongest tree feature linked to mental disorders. No source reads a rootless tree as a sign. Chapters may say art therapists ask people to draw landscapes or trees and that reading single features is weakly supported; drop the rootless-tree claim. Rows S29, S30.

## 5. Chapters 11 to 13 (items D1 to D11)

- D1 (CONFIRMED). T.S. Eliot, "Hamlet and His Problems" (written 1919, collected in The Sacred Wood, 1920): the only way to express emotion in art is to find an "objective correlative", a set of objects, a situation or a chain of events that becomes the formula of that particular emotion. Write that Eliot made the term famous ("mempopulerkan"), not that he coined it. Rows S32, S33.
- D2 (CONFIRMED, narrowed). Emily Brontë, Wuthering Heights (1847), chapter IX: Catherine says her love for Linton is like the foliage in the woods, which time will change, while her love for Heathcliff resembles the eternal rocks beneath, and she says she is Heathcliff; Linton's soul differs from Heathcliff's as a moonbeam from lightning, or frost from fire. Her dream ends with her flung onto the heath at the top of Wuthering Heights, where she wakes sobbing for joy. Chapter VIII contrasts a bleak, hilly coal country with a beautiful fertile valley when comparing Heathcliff and Linton as people. The Heights versus Grange contrast may be described as the novel's setting (one house high on the moor, one sheltered in the valley park) without a quoted passage. Row S34.
- D3 (CONFIRMED). Same chapter IX: Heathcliff overhears part of Catherine's speech and leaves; around midnight a thunderstorm breaks, a split bough knocks down part of the east chimney stack, and Catherine stays out in the rain looking for him. Nelly later dates his disappearance to "the evening of the thunder-storm". Row S34.
- D4 (PARTLY CONFIRMED). Ahmad Tohari, Ronggeng Dukuh Paruk (Gramedia; the publisher's author note dates its first Gramedia edition to 1981, so chapters omit the year or say "awal 1980-an"): the novel opens in a long dry season, the sawah dry for seven months; Ki Secamenggala, the village ancestor, lies in a grave on a small hill shaded by a beringin, the centre of the village's spiritual life; Srintil becomes the ronggeng; before the bongkrek poisoning, omens appear (wilting puring leaves, frangipani blooming out of season) and the kedasih bird calls from nightfall to dawn. "Pohon pule" is not in the text; drop it. The facts were checked against an unofficial online copy that must not be linked or quoted; cite the Gramedia page and the Mukhlis (2023) ecocritical article (DOI 10.24235/ileal.v8i2.11655), which reads the trilogy's environment and ecological message. Rows S35, S36.
- D5 (CONFIRMED). Hemingway, The Old Man and the Sea (Scribner's, 1952): Santiago, an ageing fisherman, hooks a giant marlin far out in the Gulf Stream after a long run without a catch; sharks strip it on the way home and only the skeleton reaches harbour; his credo that a man can be destroyed but not defeated. It won the 1953 Pulitzer Prize for fiction. Row S37 (plus B12 in section 3).
- D6 (CONFIRMED). Wordsworth, "Lines Composed a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey" (dated 13 July 1798): nature never betrayed the heart that loved her. Row S38.
- D7 (CONFIRMED). Edmund Burke, A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful (1757): danger and pain are terrible when they press close, but at a certain distance they can be delightful, and that delight is the sublime; vastness and infinity are among its causes. Romanticism in art and letters flourished from the late 18th century until about the middle of the 19th (Met Museum essay). Rows S39, S40.
- D8 (CONFIRMED). William Rueckert, "Literature and Ecology: An Experiment in Ecocriticism", Iowa Review 9.1 (1978); Cheryll Glotfelty and Harold Fromm (eds.), The Ecocriticism Reader (University of Georgia Press, 1996), which includes Rueckert's essay. Write that the term "ecocriticism" appears in Rueckert's 1978 essay title; do not claim he coined it. Rows S41, S42.
- D9 (CORRECTED). "Cli-fi" (climate fiction) is credited to journalist Dan Bloom, who began using it in 2007 or 2008; it entered mainstream media around 2013. Rows S43, S44.
- D10 (CONFIRMED). Coleridge, The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (first published 1798 in Lyrical Ballads; the checked text is 1834): the mariner shoots the albatross with a crossbow; the ship lies becalmed under a hot copper sky and a bloody sun, with water everywhere and not a drop to drink; after the crew blame him, the albatross is hung about his neck. Row S45.
- D11 (CORRECTED). Cormac McCarthy, The Road (Knopf, 2006), Pulitzer Prize for Fiction 2007: a father and son walk through a burned America, ash on the wind, under a dark sky, with nearly all life gone. Do not write "no living plants" or "the sun is never seen" as fact. Rows S46, S47.
