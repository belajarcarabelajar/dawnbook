## 1. Method

- Base text: the owner's own study notes on the topic (12 modules, a local Markdown file outside this repository, read through the environment variable named in the book test). Chapter authors read their part there; these notes do not copy it. The notes carry no references.
- Every factual claim the chapters may keep was checked by four read-only researchers (aqua x2, timid x2; 55, 60, 72 and 73 tool calls) with TinyFish `search` and `fetch_content` on 2026-11-15, plus three parent fetches (Diogenes Laertius on Perseus, the Donner party page of the U.S. National Park Service, an Open Library record). Each item gives the verified fact in paraphrase and its source row (section 9).
- Status words: CONFIRMED (state it), CORRECTED (state the corrected fact directly, with no mention of the old wording), HEDGE (state it with the hedge given), DROP and UNVERIFIED (leave it out of the book; an invented example may stay only as a clearly hypothetical scene with no factual claim).
- etymonline.com and most Britannica pages refused automated fetches, so they are not cited. Rows marked "Wikipedia fallback" are the best fetched source for that fact.
- Greek is written in Greek script with an italic transliteration (never inside `$...$`). Quotes from foreign texts are paraphrased in Indonesian.

## 2. Chapters 01 to 03 (items A1 to A31)

### Chapter 01, the root anthropos

- A1 (CORRECTED, hedge). ἀνήρ (*anēr*) means an adult man, a husband, and also a mortal as opposed to a god; ἄνθρωπος (*anthrōpos*) means a human being, distinguished from gods and beasts, and mankind as a whole, and takes either grammatical gender. The contrast "anēr = male, anthrōpos = human" holds for the first sense of anēr only; present it as a tendency. Rows S1, S2.
- A2 (CONFIRMED, hedge). In Plato's Cratylus (399c) Socrates explains the name: other animals do not examine or look up at what they see, while man, having seen, looks up at and considers it (Greek *anathrei ha opōpe*). It is a playful etymology offered by a character in a dialogue, not a finding of linguistics. Row S3.
- A3 (CORRECTED). Plato's play on words joins "look up at, consider" with "has seen". It does not involve *anthos* (flower), and the passage says nothing about upright posture or bipedalism. Row S3.
- A4 (CONFIRMED, hedge). Modern dictionaries call the origin uncertain. The word is first attested in Mycenaean Greek as *a-to-ro-qo*. An older idea joins anēr with ōps ("face, look"), "he who looks like a man", against which a δ would be expected (genitive andros); Rosén (1986) defended the compound, Beekes thinks the word is probably Pre-Greek, and Garnier (2008) proposed a derivation meaning "earthly". Name these only as Wiktionary reports them ("menurut ringkasan Wiktionary"). Row S1.
- A5 (CONFIRMED). English "anthropology" comes from New Latin *anthropologia* (anthrōpos + -logia); "philanthropy" comes through Late Latin *philanthrōpia* from Greek *philanthrōpia*. Rows S7, S12.
- A6 (HEDGE). Poetic Greek also used βροτός (*brotos*), "mortal", from a root meaning dead or mortal. Row S4.
- A7 (CONFIRMED). "Androgyny" comes from ἀνδρόγυνος, from anēr (stem andr-) and gynē (woman), so andro- words belong to anēr, not to anthrōpos. Rows S5, S6.
- DROP: the Koine/New Testament usage claim, "anthropology entered English in the 16th century", the dog-and-rainbow analogy as a fact, any posture-based etymology.

### Chapter 02, anthropology

- A11 (CONFIRMED). The American Anthropological Association describes four fields in the US (human biology, archaeology, cultural anthropology, linguistics) as the pillars of the discipline; an anthropologist usually specialises in one or two but knows all four. Wiktionary describes anthropology as holistic, combining ethnographic, linguistic, archaeological and evolutionary dimensions. Rows S8, S7.
- A12 (CORRECTED, see E14). The Universitas Terbuka module on the scope of anthropology, citing Koentjaraningrat, names two branches, physical anthropology and cultural anthropology, with five subfields; set it next to the AAA four fields.
- A13 (CORRECTED). Two lines often attached to Ruth Benedict are of doubtful attribution; attribute neither to her. Safe fact: Benedict wrote Patterns of Culture (1934), listed in the Stanford Encyclopedia entry on moral relativism, which treats the anthropological background of relativism. Row S9.
- A14 (HEDGE). Cultural relativism, understanding a practice within its own cultural context, may be explained in the author's words with a pointer to S9; no dictionary definition is quoted.
- A15 (CONFIRMED). Anthropology stresses in-depth context, comparison and long-term experiential immersion (ethnography). No figure for months or years. Row S7.
- A18 (CONFIRMED, hedge). In the 2014 Ebola outbreak in Liberia, families washed and touched the bodies of the dead; these cherished rituals contributed to the spread before they were stopped, and anthropologists such as Alain Epelboin helped the WHO design safer burials that respect local traditions (PBS NewsHour, 14 October 2014). A WHO Africa page names social anthropologist Julienne Anoko encouraging safe and dignified burial in the DR Congo. Write "ikut menyebarkan" and "membantu merancang", never "the main route" or "succeeded". Rows S10, S11.
- DROP: the cranial-capacity formula (owner decision: no formulas), "Google or Intel hire anthropologists", the beef/snail example as a fact, "diversity is our survival strategy".

### Chapter 03, philanthropy

- A20 (CONFIRMED). φιλανθρωπία comes from φιλάνθρωπος, "loving mankind". Rows S13, S12.
- A21 (CORRECTED). In Prometheus Bound, traditionally attributed to Aeschylus, the adjective appears at line 11: Power (Kratos) says Prometheus must learn to cease his man-loving ways (*philanthrōpou tropou*), and Hephaestus uses the phrase again at line 28. It is an early attested use spoken as a reproach; never call it the first appearance. Rows S14, S15.
- A22 (HEDGE). Dictionaries define philanthropy broadly (goodwill to humankind, and also charitable giving). The charity-versus-philanthropy split (relief now versus root causes) is a framing common among practitioners, not a definition. Rows S12, S16.
- A23 (HEDGE). "Time, talent, treasure" is a phrase common in church stewardship and fundraising; no origin or date. No row; no "rumus 3T".
- A24 (CONFIRMED). Britannica's short history: an endowment supported Plato's Academy (c. 387 BCE) for some 900 years; the Islamic waqf dates from the 7th century CE; medieval churches administered charitable trusts; large private foundations followed the fortunes of the late 19th century. The Rockefeller Foundation was founded in 1913. Rows S16, S17.
- A25 (CONFIRMED). Matthew Bishop of The Economist coined "philanthrocapitalism" in a 2006 article; with Michael Green he wrote the book Philanthrocapitalism (Bloomsbury Press, 2008). It names rich people applying business methods to giving. No day of the month. Rows S18, S19.
- A26 (CONFIRMED). The Giving Pledge was founded by Warren Buffett, Melinda French Gates and Bill Gates and launched in 2010: a promise by very wealthy people to give the majority of their wealth to charitable causes in their lifetime or will. Row S20.
- A27 (CONFIRMED). Alfatih Timur founded Kitabisa.com, a donation-based crowdfunding platform launched in 2013. No current figures. Row S21.
- A28 (CORRECTED). Allan Luks introduced "helper's high" in Psychology Today (October 1988) from a survey of more than 1,700 women who regularly helped others, who reported a sense of calm and better self-worth. No hormone list (no dopamine, oxytocin) and no health multiplier. Row S22.
- A29 (CONFIRMED). Andreoni (1990) modelled "warm-glow giving", the pleasure of giving itself. Harbaugh, Mayr and Burghart (2007) scanned participants with fMRI and found reward-region activity when money went to a food bank, both through mandatory taxation and voluntary giving. Moll et al. (2006) found the mesolimbic reward system engaged by donations as it is by monetary rewards. These support "giving engages reward circuitry", nothing more. Rows S23, S24, S25 (bibliographic records), S118, S119 (the pages that carry the findings; the Oregon release says 19 women were scanned).
- DROP: the unattributed definition quote, "the highest evolution of human consciousness", the digital-era framing of philanthrocapitalism, the water-crisis scenario as a real case (it may stay as a hypothetical), religious-giving history without a source.

## 3. Chapters 04 to 06 (items B1 to B35)

### Chapter 04, misanthropy

- B1 (CORRECTED). μισάνθρωπος comes from μῖσος (*mîsos*, hatred) and anthrōpos; the verb μισέω (*miseō*, to hate) shares the root. Rows S26, S27, S28.
- B3 (CORRECTED). Diogenes Laertius says Diogenes of Sinope lit a lamp in broad daylight and said "I am looking for a man"; the "honest man" version is a later tradition (Britannica: "tradition ascribes"). Rows S29, S30.
- B4 (CONFIRMED). He made the tub in the Metroön his home. Row S29.
- B5 (HEDGE). Diogenes is the archetype of the Cynics, mocking convention and luxury; Laertius says the Athenians loved him. Do not call him a misanthrope; he mocked human conventions. Died c. 320 BCE. Rows S29, S30.
- B6 (HEDGE). Schopenhauer, The Wisdom of Life (Saunders translation): the wise man seeks a quiet, modest life with few encounters and, after experience of his fellows, may choose retirement or solitude; the more a man has in himself, the less he needs from others. The Stanford Encyclopedia calls him often considered a thorough pessimist who nevertheless proposed ways out of suffering. Not a plain misanthrope. Rows S31, S32.
- B7 (CORRECTED). Molière's Le Misanthrope, a five-act comedy, was performed in 1666 and published in 1667; Alceste is a painfully forthright gentleman who cannot bear the flattery and hypocrisy of polite society. No court setting. Row S33.
- B8 (HEDGE). In the 1667 text Alceste speaks of sudden urges to flee to a desert, and at the end announces he will seek a secluded spot where a man may be honest; the play ends on that intention. Rows S34, S33.
- B11 (CONFIRMED). Antisocial personality disorder is a DSM-5-TR diagnosis marked by disregard for the rights of others and unlawful, impulsive behaviour; misanthropy is an attitude, not a diagnosis (author's statement, no citation). Row S35.
- DROP: Sherlock Holmes and Gregory House as misanthropes, ecological "virus" movements, the φίλος gloss table row.

### Chapter 05, anthropomorphism

- B13 (CONFIRMED). "Anthropomorphism" was coined in the mid-1700s from anthrōpos and μορφή (*morphē*, form, shape; of unclear origin); it means ascribing human traits to non-human things, including gods. Rows S36, S37.
- B17 (CONFIRMED). Xenophanes (fragments B14 to B16, Stanford Encyclopedia): mortals imagine gods born, clothed and bodied like themselves; Ethiopians make their gods snub-nosed and dark, Thracians blue-eyed and red-haired; if horses and oxen could draw, their gods would look like horses and oxen (probably satirical). Row S38.
- B14 (HEDGE). Epley, Waytz and Cacioppo (2007): anthropomorphism is common but not invariant, shaped by three factors (accessible human-centred knowledge, effectance motivation, sociality motivation), and more likely when people lack social connection. Never "universal". Row S39.
- B15 (HEDGE). Premack and Woodruff (1978) defined theory of mind as imputing mental states to oneself and others, asking whether chimpanzees have one. No "innate" or "overactive" claim. Row S40.
- B16 (CONFIRMED). Pareidolia: perceiving a meaningful image, such as a dog in the clouds, in a random or ambiguous pattern. Row S41.
- B18 (CONFIRMED). Horowitz (2009), 14 dogs: the "guilty look" appeared no more after disobedience than after obedience, and more when owners scolded, so it answers the owner's cues rather than showing awareness of a misdeed. Rows S120 (abstract), S42 (bibliographic record).
- B19 (HEDGE). Masahiro Mori's 1970 essay proposed the uncanny valley: affinity for a humanlike robot rises, then drops into revulsion as it comes close to but fails to reach a lifelike look. A hypothesis. Row S43.
- B20 (CONFIRMED). Windhager et al. (2008): people attribute traits and emotions to car fronts, and the car features covary with these judgements much as in human faces. Nothing about sports cars looking angry. Row S44.
- B22 (HEDGE). McConnell et al. (2011): pet owners fared better on several well-being measures and pets acted as social support; this is about pet ownership, not anthropomorphism. Row S45.
- DROP: robots given faces "to trigger empathy", mascots' effect on brand ties, conservation favouring look-alike species; illustrations (a talking car in a film, a smart speaker) may stay as hypothetical scenes.

### Chapter 06, anthropocentrism

- B25 (HEDGE). κέντρον (*kentron*) first meant a sharp point, a goad or sting, the fixed point of a pair of compasses, and in geometry the centre of a circle. Row S46.
- B26 (CONFIRMED). "Anthropocentric": placing humans at the centre, preferring humans above all other considerations; "anthropocentrism" dates from about 1905 to 1910. Rows S47, S48.
- B27 (CONFIRMED, hedge). Protagoras, fragment 80B1 DK, opening his Truth: of all things the measure is man, of those that are, that they are, and of those that are not, that they are not. Its meaning is disputed; it is often read as an early stance for relativism, not as environmental ethics. Row S49.
- B29 (HEDGE). Bryan Norton (1984) distinguished strong and weak anthropocentrism and argued that weak anthropocentrism can support an environmental ethic. The Stanford Encyclopedia describes prudential anthropocentrism: caring for nature because human well-being depends on it. Rows S50, S51.
- B30 (HEDGE). Biocentrism (Paul Taylor, 1981 and 1986): every living thing has a good of its own and deserves respect (duties, not "rights"). Ecocentrism draws on Leopold's land community (1949). Row S51.
- B31 (CORRECTED). Richard Ryder coined "speciesism" in the 1970s for a human-centred prejudice like racism; Peter Singer popularised it. Rows S52, S51.
- B32 (CORRECTED). Lynn White Jr. (1967) argued that strands of Judeo-Christian thought encouraged exploitation of nature by asserting human superiority; it is a widely discussed thesis about an ecological crisis, not climate change, and not a proven cause. Rows S53, S51.
- B35 (CONFIRMED). Arne Naess (1973) distinguished shallow and deep ecology movements. Rows S54, S51.
- DROP: "Antroposentrisme Terencana", "chauvinisme spesies", the reason/religion/moral-agency foundations as sourced claims. The dam case stays a clearly hypothetical scene with no real figures.

## 4. Chapters 07 to 09 (items C1 to C33)

### Chapter 07, anthropoids (with paleoanthropology)

- C1 (CONFIRMED). "Anthropoid" comes from ἀνθρωποειδής, anthrōpos + -eidēs (like, in form): resembling a human, or an ape. Row S55.
- C2 (CORRECTED). Anthropoidea and Simiiformes are two names for one group, today an infraorder of the haplorhine primates; older schemes made Anthropoidea a suborder opposite the prosimians; which name has priority is still argued. Row S56 (Wikipedia fallback).
- C3 (CORRECTED). Simians split into New World monkeys (Platyrrhini) and Old World monkeys and apes (Catarrhini). Gibbons are lesser apes; the great apes are chimpanzees, gorillas, orangutans and humans. Humans are apes (Hominoidea), with no "peak" ranking. Rows S56, S57.
- C4 (CORRECTED). Tarsiers sit with the simians in Haplorhini, so "prosimian" is no longer the opposite of "anthropoid". Row S56.
- C5 (CORRECTED, hedge). The 2005 chimpanzee genome paper: single-nucleotide differences average about 1.23%, with fixed differences 1.06% or less; insertions and deletions add about 1.5% of sequence specific to each lineage. The Broad Institute: almost 99% identical on directly comparable sequence, about 96% when insertions and deletions count; the lineages split about 6 million years ago. The figure depends on the method. Rows S58, S59. The Broad release (S59, fetched 2026-11-15) calls chimpanzees "our closest living relatives"; ch07 says "kerabat terdekat manusia yang masih hidup".
- C6 (CORRECTED). Humans have 23 chromosome pairs, the other great apes 24; human chromosome 2 formed from the fusion of two ancestral ape chromosomes. Gibbons have other numbers. Rows S60, S57.
- C7 (HEDGE). Whiten et al. (1999) pooled seven long-term chimpanzee studies (151 years of observation) and found 39 behaviour patterns customary in some communities and absent in others. No specific tool examples. Row S61.
- C8 (HEDGE). Anderson, Gillies and Lock (2010) described a group's care for a dying elderly female chimpanzee, her daughter staying the night, and the place avoided afterwards, explicitly without death-related symbols or rituals. Never "ritual duka". Row S62.
- C10 (CONFIRMED). Paleoanthropology: palaios (old) + anthrōpos + -logia; a branch of paleontology and anthropology that reconstructs human evolution from fossil bones, footprints, stone tools and sites, drawing on primatology, biological and cultural anthropology and, increasingly, genetics. Rows S63, S64, S65.
- DROP: flat face, diurnal, binocular vision, opposable thumb, brain ratio lists as sourced claims; the claim that the small DNA gap explains language and technology; medical-research and conservation claims.

### Chapter 08, the Anthropocene (with anthropogenic)

- C11 (CONFIRMED). Anthropocene = anthropo- + -cene, modelled on Holocene and Pleistocene; -cene comes from καινός (*kainos*, new) and marks a division of the Cenozoic. Rows S66, S67.
- C12 (CONFIRMED, hedge). Paul Crutzen and Eugene Stoermer proposed the term in Global Change Newsletter 41 (May 2000), suggesting the late 18th century as a start, which they admitted was somewhat arbitrary, tied to ice-core records of rising greenhouse gases; Crutzen restated it in Nature (2002). Wiktionary notes earlier uses in translated Russian texts of the 1960s. Rows S69, S70, S66.
- C13 (CONFIRMED). The Holocene began about 11,700 years before AD 2000, defined in the Greenland NGRIP ice core; officially we still live in the Meghalayan Age of the Holocene. Rows S71, S68.
- C15 (HEDGE). Proposed starts include the Industrial Revolution (about 1780), the rise of farming, and the mid-20th century; the Anthropocene Working Group chose the mid-20th century, marked by plutonium from nuclear tests, with a base at 1952 in its formal report. Rows S68, S72.
- C16 (CONFIRMED). The working group's binding vote, released 21 May 2019, passed 29 to 4; in July 2023 it named Crawford Lake, Ontario, as its proposed reference site. Rows S68, S73.
- C17 (CORRECTED). In March 2024 the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy voted to reject the Anthropocene as a formal epoch, and the IUGS and ICS approved that vote; the term stays in use as a descriptor of human impact on the Earth system. The vote was disputed by the subcommission's chair. Never call it an official epoch. Rows S74, S73, S68.
- C18 (CORRECTED). The global annual mean of atmospheric carbon dioxide reached 422.8 ppm in 2024 (NOAA); ice cores show it never above 300 ppm in the last 800,000 years and about 280 ppm before the Industrial Revolution. Row S75.
- C19 (HEDGE). Technofossils are the preservable remains of the technosphere; humans make materials rare in nature in pure form, such as aluminium, at least 500 million tons of it virtually all since 1950 (Zalasiewicz et al., 2014). Row S76.
- C20 (HEDGE). All 11 snow samples from Mount Everest held microplastics, the highest concentration at base camp and some at 8,440 m (Napper et al., 2020). Row S77.
- C21 (CORRECTED, hedge). Ceballos et al. (2015): vertebrate species loss over the last century runs up to 100 times the conservative background rate; they conclude a sixth mass extinction is under way, a claim that rests on that comparison. Row S78.
- C22 (CORRECTED). The Paris Agreement (adopted 12 December 2015) aims to keep warming well below 2 °C above pre-industrial levels and to pursue 1,5 °C. It is not "based on" the Anthropocene. Row S79.
- C23 (CONFIRMED). USGS EarthWord: "anthropogenic" means environmental change caused or influenced by people, directly or indirectly; anthropo- concerns humanity and -genic refers to creation; examples include introduced snakehead fish and Burmese pythons. No first-use date. Row S80.
- DROP: concrete covering the Earth, the extinction-rate formula, the Mariana Trench claim, circular-economy and architecture claims as facts.

### Chapter 09, anthropometry

- C25 (CONFIRMED). "Anthropometry" dates from 1839, through French *anthropométrie*, from anthrōpos + μέτρον (*metron*, measure): measuring the human body to find ranges and averages, used in physical anthropology, epidemiology and ergonomics. It was also used in attempts to tie body measures to race and psychology, a history worth one sentence. Rows S81, S82.
- C27 (HEDGE). Tools include tape measures, anthropometers and 3D body scanners. Row S82.
- C28 (CONFIRMED, hedge). Alphonse Bertillon published his identification method in 1879, using five measurements (head length, head breadth, middle finger, left foot, forearm), first identified a criminal with it in 1883, and it became the first widely used scientific method of criminal identification. Row S83.
- C29 (CONFIRMED). Adolphe Quetelet's 1835 book described "the average man"; a footnote compared 12 short and 12 tall subjects of each sex and found weight roughly proportional to height squared, the ratio later called body mass index. The CDC calls BMI (kg divided by m squared) a quick, low-cost screening measure that does not measure body fat directly. Rows S84, S85, S86, S87.
- C30 (HEDGE). NASA long required hardware to fit the 5th to 95th percentile of measurements, then found many crew dimensions outside that range, so spacesuits must fit the real crew population. Row S88.
- C33 (CORRECTED). Forensic anthropologists help estimate sex, stature, age and ancestry (never "race") from the skeleton; stature can be estimated from long bones. Row S89.
- DROP: the Z = 1,645 formula, "jangka sorong" for skinfold calipers, waist-hip ratio claims, workstation and car-seat tables. A desk or doorway that fits nobody may stay as a hypothetical thought experiment.

## 5. Chapters 10 to 13 (items D1 to D54)

### Chapter 10, anthroponyms

- D1 (CONFIRMED). Anthroponymy, the study of personal names, from anthrōpos + -onymia, from ὄνομα (*onoma*, name); "anthroponym" is first attested in 1955. Rows S91, S90.
- D6 (HEDGE). In Iceland a child takes a patronymic surname (father's name in the genitive + -son or -dóttir) and in some cases a matronymic one; the registry describes the rules. Rows S92, S93.
- D9 (CORRECTED). The Minangkabau are a matrilineal society (descent and clan through the mother); that is not a matronymic surname system. Row S95.
- D10 (HEDGE). The English word smith means a metal craftsman (Old English *smiþ*); occupational surnames may be described generally. Row S99.
- D14 (CONFIRMED, hedge). Teknonymy, calling parents by their child's name, was named by Edward Burnett Tylor in 1889 (téknon, child + onoma). An Indonesian example may stay only as an everyday illustration. Row S94.
- D15 (CORRECTED). Hebrew Yohanan means "God is gracious". Row S97.
- D18 (HEDGE). Wiktionary has an entry for the place-name ending -by; any inference about its history stays hedged. Row S98.
- D19 (CONFIRMED). Robert goes back to a Germanic name meaning roughly "shining glory". Row S96.
- Surname classes and Indonesian single names: see E1 to E7.
- DROP: names as the last surviving element of a language, "bin/binti", Sudarmono, Eriksson, da Vinci, Brown, the Latin praenomen mapping, the John-to-Ivan chain, Yahya, de Silva, branding sounds, Batak marga as legal proof.

### Chapter 11, anthropophagy

- D22 (CONFIRMED). Anthropophagy = anthropo- + -phagy, from φαγεῖν (*phagein*, to eat): the eating of human flesh. Rows S100, S101.
- D23 (CONFIRMED). "Cannibal" comes through Spanish from Taíno *caniba*, the name Columbus recorded for the feared Caribs. Row S102.
- D26 (CORRECTED). Kuru, a fatal brain disease of the Fore people in the Eastern Highlands of Papua New Guinea, was first reported to Western medicine in 1957 by Gajdusek and Zigas; about 1,000 people died in 1957 to 1961; it spread through mortuary feasting and could incubate for more than 50 years; in Fore the word means to tremble from cold or fever. Rows S103, S104.
- D27 (CORRECTED). Kuru is a prion disease related to, but distinct from, bovine spongiform encephalopathy of cattle. Row S103.
- D29 (CONFIRMED). Gajdusek shared the 1976 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discoveries on the origin and spread of infectious diseases; Prusiner won in 1997 for the discovery of prions. Rows S105, S106.
- D30 (CORRECTED). Herodotus (Histories 4.106) describes the Androphagi, "man-eaters", as nomads with their own language who know no justice or law, the only people of the region who eat men. Never "first popularised the term". Row S107.
- D32 (CORRECTED, hedge). Bello's review (2025): marks on bones alone cannot tell survival from ritual cannibalism; context is needed. No named mark types. Row S108.
- D33 (CONFIRMED). The Donner-Reed party of 1846: of 87 who began the trek, 40 died that winter, some survivors ate the dead, and the rest were rescued in spring 1847. Row S109.
- D34 (CONFIRMED). Uruguayan Air Force flight 571 crashed in the Andes on 13 October 1972 with 45 aboard, including an amateur rugby team; 16 survived, after more than two months, by eating the dead. Row S110.
- Endocannibalism and exocannibalism: see E8 to E10.
- DROP: the prion equation, "laporan medis dan hukum", Neanderthal isotope and burial claims, "pot marks" and "anvil marks" definitions.

### Chapter 12, lycanthropy (no base text in the owner's notes)

- D37 (CONFIRMED). λυκάνθρωπος = λύκος (*lykos*, wolf) + anthrōpos; lycanthropy names the werewolf condition of myth and a delusion of being a wolf or another animal. Rows S111, S112.
- D38 (CONFIRMED). Lycaon was a mythical king of Arcadia turned into a wolf by Zeus. Row S113.
- D39 (CONFIRMED). Ovid, Metamorphoses book 1 (Kline translation): Jupiter tells how Lycaon mocked his divinity, plotted to kill him in his sleep, killed a hostage and served the flesh; Jupiter struck the house with fire, and Lycaon fled howling, his clothes turning to bristling hair and his arms to legs, keeping the same grey hair and violent face. Row S114.
- D40 (CONFIRMED). Petronius, Satyricon 62: Niceros tells how a soldier, walking at night under a bright moon, stripped among the tombs and became a wolf; the next day the soldier lay in bed with a doctor tending his neck, and Niceros knew he was a werewolf. Row S115.
- D41 (HEDGE). The moon in Niceros' story only lights the scene; it is no transformation rule. Row S115.
- D42 (CONFIRMED). Clinical lycanthropy is a rare delusion of turning into an animal; a 2021 systematic review found 43 cases (wolf or dog), linked with schizophrenia, psychotic depression, bipolar disorder and other psychoses, with antipsychotics possibly helping. Row S116.
- D43 (HEDGE). The rhyme in The Wolf Man (1941) was written by screenwriter Curt Siodmak and first spoke of the autumn moon; later films made the full moon the trigger. A modern film convention, not ancient lore. Row S117.

## 5b. Top-up round (items E1 to E15, structural items)

- E1 (CONFIRMED). The Library of Congress surname guide lists four origins of surnames: occupation, nicknames, place names (toponymics), and naming patterns with patronymics; surnames were uncommon in Europe before the Middle Ages; onomastics is the study of the history and origin of personal names. There is no separate "descriptive" class: physical traits fall under nicknames. Row S121.
- E2 (CONFIRMED). Many countries have surnames meaning "son of" or "daughter of", in some places changing every generation; Johnson is a patronymic, John + -son, "son of John". Rows S121, S124.
- E4 (CONFIRMED). Occupational surnames include Miller, Smith and Tailor (Library of Congress); Wiktionary calls Smith an English surname originating as an occupation and Baker one for a baker or owner of a communal oven. No "iron" gloss for Smith. Rows S121, S122, S123.
