# Research notes: a book on handling romantic interest you do not return

Session: dawnbook-menolak-perasaan. Plan: docs/code-plan/plans/2026-10-19-dawnbook-menolak-perasaan.md.
Method: 6 researcher subagents (sets A-F) plus one gap wave (G1-G3) and two parent fetches, all retrieval through TinyFish `search` and `fetch_content`. Every URL below was accessed on 2026-10-19. Confidence: H = read on a primary or reputable page (often the abstract only, stated per item); M = secondary report, author manuscript, or partial read; U = unverified, do not use as a fact in a chapter.

Rules for chapter authors:
- Use only facts marked H or M. Phrase M facts with attribution ("menurut laporan ...", "dalam ringkasan ..."). Never use a U item.
- Most papers were read at abstract level. Do not add sample sizes, effect sizes, or details that are not written here.
- Every Referensi entry must be a URL from the source table (section 9).
- The topic was chosen by the owner. Chapter scenarios are invented (section 8).

## 1. Unrequited love and the rejecter's side (set A)

- A1 (H, abstract). Baumeister, R. F., Wotman, S. R., & Stillwell, A. M. (1993). Unrequited love: On heartbreak, anger, guilt, scriptlessness, and humiliation. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 64(3), 377-394. Narrative accounts from both sides. Unrequited love is "a bilaterally distressing experience marked by mutual incomprehension and emotional interdependence." Rejecters "depicted themselves as morally innocent but still felt guilty about hurting someone"; many saw the would-be lover's "persistent efforts as intrusive and annoying"; they built accounts to reduce guilt. Would-be lovers looked back with "both positive and intensely negative emotions", "believed that the attraction had been mutual, that they had been led on, and that the rejection had never been communicated definitely", built accounts "to rebuild self-esteem", and saw rejecters "as inconsistent and mysterious". The abstract does not define "scriptlessness"; only the title names it, so a chapter may say the title lists it, not explain it as a finding.
- A2 (H, abstract). Joel, S., Teper, R., & MacDonald, G. (2014). People overestimate their willingness to reject potential romantic partners by overlooking their concern for other people. Psychological Science, 25(12), 2233-2240. Two studies; single people facing advances from potential dates who were unattractive (Study 1) or incompatible with their preferences (Study 2) "were significantly less willing to reject these unsuitable potential dates when they believed the situation to be real rather than hypothetical." Partly explained by "other-focused motives": hypothetical participants anticipated less motivation to avoid hurting the other's feelings than real participants felt. Press release quote (ScienceDaily, 25 Nov 2014, M): "When actually faced with a potential date, we don't like to reject a person and make them feel bad, which is not necessarily something that people anticipate when they imagine making these choices." No sample sizes.
- A3 (H, full text via archive). Bringle, R. G., Winnick, T., & Rydell, R. J. (2013). The prevalence and nature of unrequited love. SAGE Open, 3(2). Study 1a: 318 high school and university students (aged 14 to 63, mean 21,1). In the past 2 years, 88% had at least one instance of some type of unrequited love; 70% had equal (mutual) romantic love; "instances of all types of UL were over 4 times more prevalent than equal love." Types and share with at least one instance: crush on a star 48%, crush on someone nearby 63%, pursuing someone 36%, longing for a past lover 40%, unequal love 57%. About 1,91 instances of all types per year. Study 2: unrequited love was less intense than equal love on passion, commitment and similar scales, but more intense on turmoil. Limits: convenience sample of students; it measures the would-be lover side and includes crushes on celebrities.
- A4 (H citation and abstract; sample sizes U). Folkes, V. S. (1982). Communicating the reasons for social rejection. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 18(3), 235-252. Study One (accounts of turning down a date): rejecters "often reported withholding the reason for rejection"; stated reasons were "primarily impersonal, uncontrollable, and unstable." Study Two: reasons tied to the appearance or personality of the rejected person were less likely to be revealed. The abstract reads this as self-presentation, "reflecting concerns about rejected persons' emotional reactions, expectancies for the future of the relationship, and blame for rejection." Do not use any percentage from this paper.
- A5 (H for the article; theory paper, no data). Freedman, G., Williams, K. D., & Beer, J. S. (2016). Softening the blow of social exclusion: The responsive theory of social exclusion. Frontiers in Psychology, 7, Article 1570. Sources of rejection have three options: "explicit rejection (clearly stating no), ostracism (ignoring), and ambiguous rejection (being unclear)." The theory predicts "targets and sources' needs are better maintained if sources use clear, explicit verbal communication", with targets' feelings less hurt and sources experiencing "less backlash and emotional toil". Example of explicit rejection: "I've had fun talking to you, but I don't want to go to lunch with you." State this as a theory prediction, not a proven result.
- A6 (M, practitioner page reviewed by a counselor). Verywell Mind, "Rejecting Someone Can Be Done Nicely" (Sanjana Gupta, reviewed by Divya Robin, LMHC; updated 16 Sep 2026). Be kind and direct, avoid false hope and clichés, use "I" statements, thank the person, keep it brief; "'no' is a complete sentence" (attributed to Dr. Sabrina Romanoff). Avoid "maybe later", ghosting, and "it's not you, it's me". If the person keeps pressing after a clear no, it is fine to be firmer and to block them across platforms.
- A7 (M, Indonesian health portal interviewing a psychologist). Klikdokter, "Cara Menolak Cinta dengan Halus Menurut Psikolog" (Tri Yuniwati Lestari, 20 Mar 2022, with Ikhsan Bella Persada, M.Psi., Psikolog). Four tips: show appreciation, give a sincere compliment, speak honestly, accept that the other person will be hurt. Avoid promises that keep the person waiting without certainty, because false hope makes them hope for more. It is fine to say you can stay friends so the other person knows the refusal concerns romance, not the friendship. Paraphrase only (the page addresses the reader as "Anda").

## 2. Indirect signals, flirting, and compliments (set B)

- B1 (H, full text). Pinker, S., Nowak, M. A., & Lee, J. J. (2008). The logic of indirect speech. PNAS, 105(3), 833-838. A Perspective with no experiments. Indirect requests "allow for plausible deniability, in which a cooperative listener can accept the request, but an uncooperative one cannot react adversarially to it." Sexual come-ons are the first example ("Would you like to come up and see my etchings?"). Indirectness avoids "the emotional cost of a relationship mismatch"; the authors predict it makes it "easier for the participants to resume their normal relationship should the offer be rebuffed" (a stated hypothesis, not tested there). A blunt overture creates common knowledge that cannot be taken back. "The deniability, then, doesn't have to be plausible, only possible."
- B2 (H, abstract). Lee, J. J., & Pinker, S. (2010). Rationales for indirect speech: The theory of the strategic speaker. Psychological Review, 117(3), 785-807. The strategic speaker "seeks plausible deniability when he or she is uncertain of whether the hearer is cooperative or antagonistic"; everyday exchanges can carry "emotional rather than legal penalties"; even when willingness is known, indirect speech "offers higher-order plausible deniability by preempting certainty, gossip, and common knowledge of the request." No numbers.
- B3 (H citation; styles via a later paper). Hall, J. A., Carter, S., Cody, M. J., & Albright, J. M. (2010). Individual differences in the communication of romantic interest: Development of the Flirting Styles Inventory. Communication Quarterly, 58(4), 365-393. Five styles: physical, traditional, sincere, polite, playful (named in B4's abstract).
- B4 (H, abstract). Hall, J. A., & Xing, C. (2015). The verbal and nonverbal correlates of the five flirting styles. Journal of Nonverbal Behavior, 39(1), 41-68. 51 pairs (N = 102) of strangers in 10 to 12 minute conversations. Polite flirts showed "more reserved and distancing behavior"; playful flirts "more obviously engaging and flirtatious behaviors"; sincere flirts more smiling and less fidgeting.
- B5 (M, university news release about Hall's book, 11 Sep 2013). Playful flirts enjoy "flirting for its own sake, even when a relationship or hookup is the last thing on their mind"; polite flirts can take "forever to get a message across"; physical flirts are "more likely to assume the person they're flirting with is interested when they're not"; sincere flirts seek emotional connection. Do not use the eHarmony sample figures.
- B6 (H, abstract). Henningsen, D. D. (2004). Flirting with meaning: An examination of miscommunication in flirting interactions. Sex Roles, 50, 481-489. Six flirting motivations: "sex, fun, exploring, relational, esteem, and instrumental." "Men tend to view flirting as more sexual than women do, and women attribute more relational and fun motivations to flirting interactions than do men." No gender differences for esteem, exploring, instrumental. Framed as a source of miscommunication.
- B7 (H, abstract). Abbey, A. (1982). Sex differences in attributions for friendly behavior: Do males misperceive females' friendliness? JPSP, 42(5), 830-838. Lab study, 36 sessions, 144 undergraduates, 5-minute conversations with hidden observers. Male actors and observers rated the female actor as more "promiscuous and seductive" than female actors and observers did. Small lab student sample, 1982.
- B8 (H abstract; M numbers from the authors' manuscript). Zhao, X., & Epley, N. (2021). Insufficiently complimentary?: Underestimating the positive impact of compliments creates a barrier to expressing them. JPSP, 121(2), 239-256. "Nine experiments"; expressers "underestimated how positive recipients would feel but overestimated how awkward recipients would feel." Experiment 1 (manuscript): expected positive mood 7,79 vs actual 8,81; expected awkwardness 3,46 vs actual 2,06; reading compliments did not make recipients feel more awkward than controls. Participants knew each other (friends, family, couples), so applying it to an unwanted admirer is an inference.
- B9 (H, abstract). Boothby, E. J., & Bohns, V. K. (2021). Why a simple act of kindness is not as simple as it seems: Underestimating the positive impact of our compliments on others. Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 47(5), 826-840. Five studies: givers underestimated how positively the recipient would feel and "overestimated how bothered and uncomfortable the recipient would feel", "even in hindsight"; the error came from givers' own anxiety about their competence; givers "felt better after having done so."

## 3. Ghosting, silence, and the rejected side (set C)

- C1 (H, abstract). LeFebvre, L. E., Allen, M., Rasner, R. D., Garstad, S., Wilms, A., & Parrish, C. (2019). Ghosting in emerging adults' romantic relationships: The digital dissolution disappearance strategy. Imagination, Cognition and Personality, 39(2), 125-150. Qualitative, N = 99 emerging adults; "the majority of participants reported participating in both roles" (ghoster and ghosted). Five themes for why initiators ghost (names U).
- C2 (H, full text, author copy). Freedman, G., Powell, D. N., Le, B., & Williams, K. D. (2019). Ghosting and destiny: Implicit theories of relationships predict beliefs about ghosting. Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, 36(3), 905-924. Study 1 (N = 554): 25,3% had been ghosted by a romantic partner, 21,7% had ghosted one. Study 2 (N = 747): romantic 23,0% ghosted, 18,9% ghosters; friendships 38,6% ghosted, 31,7% ghosters. "Approximately one quarter of the sample reported being ghosted and about one fifth reported ghosting a former romantic partner." Stronger "destiny" beliefs go with more acceptance and use of ghosting; "growth" beliefs with less. US crowdsourced samples, self-report.
- C3 (H, abstract and results excerpts). Koessler, R. B., Kohut, T., & Campbell, L. (2019). When your boo becomes a ghost: The association between breakup strategy and breakup role in experiences of relationship dissolution. Collabra: Psychology, 5(1), Article 29. Relationships that ended through ghosting "were shorter and characterized by lower commitment"; "Recipients experienced greater distress and negative affect than disengagers, and ghosting disengagers reported less distress than direct disengagers." The paper does not show that ghosted recipients suffered more than directly rejected ones.
- C4 (H, abstract). Pancani, L., Mazzoni, D., Aureli, N., & Riva, P. (2021). Ghosting and orbiting: An analysis of victims' experiences. Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, 38(7), 1987-2007. 208 young adults; reaction stages "1) surprise and confusion, 2) anger, sadness, and guilt, 3) attempts of relational repair, 4) acceptance." Ghosting and orbiting "can be considered forms of ostracism."
- C5 (H, abstract and discussion). Pancani, L., Aureli, N., & Riva, P. (2022). Relationship dissolution strategies: Comparing the psychological consequences of ghosting, orbiting, and rejection. Cyberpsychology, 16(2), Article 9. N = 176; "ghosting led to worse outcomes than rejection"; emotions "did not vary across conditions" but "feelings of exclusion were stronger for ghosted than rejected participants." Evidence on whether ghosting hurts more than direct rejection is mixed (C3 vs C5); chapters must say so.
- C6 (H, abstract). Eisenberger, N. I., Lieberman, M. D., & Williams, K. D. (2003). Does rejection hurt? An fMRI study of social exclusion. Science, 302(5643), 290-292. In a virtual ball-tossing game, the anterior cingulate cortex was "more active during exclusion than during inclusion and correlated positively with self-reported distress"; the right ventral prefrontal cortex correlated negatively with distress. No sample size.
- C7 (H). Williams, K. D., & Nida, S. A. (2011). Ostracism: Consequences and coping. Current Directions in Psychological Science, 20, 71-75. "During the immediate stage, ostracism is felt as pain and as a threat to four fundamental needs: belonging, self-esteem, control, and meaningful existence." Stages: "immediate (or reflexive), coping (or reflective), and long-term (or resignation)." Who ostracizes and why matters little to the immediate pain, more in the coping stage. Long-term version (Williams 2009, abstract): prolonged exposure leads "eventually to resignation, alienation, helplessness, and depression."
- C8 (H abstract, hedged). Freedman, G., Hales, A. H., Powell, D. N., Le, B., & Williams, K. D. (2022). The role of gender and safety concerns in romantic rejection decisions. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 102, Article 104368. "safety concerns may make individuals more likely to engage in ghosting"; rejection can be "an interaction that can end in violence, particularly for women rejecting men". Do not claim ghosting is proven safer.
- C9 (M, review). Powell, D. N., & Freedman, G. (2024). Ghosting out of romantic relationships. NCFR Report, Summer 2024. In samples with a wide age range "approximately 50% of participants have ghosting experience." Personal reasons include "protection (e.g., avoiding hurt feelings, fearing retaliation)" and the "ease of ghosting". "it is not known whether ghosting may be a safer strategy, especially given that ghostees often try to make contact for clarity or closure." Ghostees report less control, belonging and meaningful existence and lower self-esteem. Suggests "creating and tailoring scripts for rejecting" could help.
- C10 (H, small qualitative). Campaioli, G., Testoni, I., & Zamperini, A. (2022). Double blue ticks: Reframing ghosting as ostracism through an abductive study on affordances. Cyberpsychology, 16(5), Article 10. 23 interviews, ages 21 to 34, Italy. Ghosting felt like "limbo"; lack of closure was described as "more upsetting than explicit dissolution"; even ghosters "would prefer an 'ugly truth' to uncertainty."

## 4. Online-only acquaintances, trust, and safety (set D, G1)

- D1 (H for stages 1-3). Whitty, M. T. (2013). The scammers persuasive techniques model: Development of a stage model to explain the online dating romance scam. British Journal of Criminology, 53(4), 665-684. Twenty interviewees (financial and non-financial victims). "Stage 1: Motivated to find the ideal partner." "Stage 2: Presented with the ideal profile." "Stage 3: Grooming process." The scam develops "enough trust with the victim to con them out of money". During grooming, frequent morning and evening chats made the relationship routine and "more difficult to break away from". Do not name stages 4 to 7.
- D2 (H). Komnas Perempuan, CATAHU 2025 (published 23 Jun 2026 page; fact sheet 6 Mar 2026). 376.529 cases of gender-based violence against women recorded in 2025 (up 14,07%), 89,76% in the personal domain; 97 data-providing institutions; victims dominated by women aged 18 to 40. Fact sheet: among complaints to Komnas Perempuan, "Kekerasan Berbasis Gender Online (KBGO) masih menjadi bentuk kekerasan yang paling dominan. Pada tahun 2025 tercatat 1.091 kasus KBGO." In the personal domain 534 cases of violence by ex-partners and 518 of dating violence. 1.091 counts complaints to Komnas Perempuan only, not national incidence.
- D3 (H as the vendor states; vendor survey). McAfee, "Love Bytes: How AI is shaping Modern Love" (13 Feb 2024). 7.000 adults in 7 countries, January 2024. Nearly one in four Americans enhanced dating photos or content with AI; 64% would not trust a love interest who used AI-generated photos; 57% would be "hurt or offended" if a Valentine's message was written by AI; only 24% are sure they can tell an AI-written message; nearly one-third of Americans said an online love interest turned out to be a scammer. Tips: talk to someone you trust about a new love interest, never send money to someone you have not met. Scammers "quickly move the conversation elsewhere, such as chat apps like WhatsApp and Telegram".
- D4 (M, news report of Match and Kinsey Institute survey). Mashable (10 Jun 2025): about 5.000 US singles; 26% use AI to enhance their dating lives (up 333% from 2024); nearly half of Gen Z singles have used AI in dating (profiles, messages, or screening); 36% consider using AI to generate conversations a dealbreaker.
- D5 (H, abstract). Cupach, W. R., & Spitzberg, B. H. (2000). Obsessive relational intrusion: Incidence, perceived severity, and coping. Violence and Victims, 15(4), 357-372. ORI is "repeated and unwanted pursuit and invasion of one's sense of physical or symbolic privacy by another person, either stranger or acquaintance, who desires and/or presumes an intimate relationship." Each of 63 ORI behaviors was experienced by "3-78% of respondents"; four types: "pursuit, violation, threat, and hyper-intimacy". Women rated ORI behaviors as more upsetting and threatening than men did.
- G1 (H). RAINN, "Tips for Safer Dating: Online & IRL" (last updated 31 May 2026). Take time to know someone first and meet only when comfortable; pressure to meet quickly is a red flag. Tell a trusted friend or family member where you are going, who you are meeting, and when you expect to return; share location. Meet in a well-lit, busy public place; do not go to each other's homes before trust is built. Arrange your own transport and an exit plan. Do not share home address, workplace, or daily routine. Research your match. Never send money. Resist moving the conversation off the platform too soon. "If something makes you uncomfortable, listen to your gut. Stop communicating, block the person, and report any troubling behavior." "You don't owe anyone your time or politeness when your safety or comfort is at risk." The US emergency number does not apply to Indonesia.
- Supporting link to A1: would-be lovers believed the rejection "had never been communicated definitely". That, plus A6 and A7, is the basis for saying a vague refusal can keep hope alive. No study here measured that an ambiguous refusal causes persistence; phrase it as a risk, not a law.

## 5. Harassment in public, school-age romance, and emotional maturity (set E)

- E1 (H). Koalisi Ruang Publik Aman (KRPA), "Survei Pelecehan di Ruang Publik (2019)". Open online survey, 25 Nov to 10 Dec 2018, 62.224 respondents from 34 provinces. "3 dari 5 Perempuan pernah mengalami pelecehan di ruang publik (64% dari 38.776 Perempuan)"; men 11%. Forms: verbal 60% ("komentar atas tubuh, siulan, klakson, suara kecupan / ciuman, komentar rasis / seksis, komentar seksual, didekati terus"), physical 24%, visual 15%. Places: public streets 33%, public transport 19%, "Sekolah dan Kampus" 15%. "1 dari 2 korban mengalami pelecehan sebelum menginjak usia 16 tahun". Clothing at the time included school uniforms (14%). Open survey: describes respondents, not the population.
- E2 (H). KRPA, survey 2022 page: 4.236 respondents (fieldwork 25 Nov to 24 Dec 2021), "79% dari 3.539 Perempuan"; offline forms led by "Siulan / Suitan (67%), Komentar atas tubuh (31%)".
- E3 (M; text from an official-text mirror, metadata from BPK). Undang-Undang Nomor 12 Tahun 2022 tentang Tindak Pidana Kekerasan Seksual (in force 9 Mei 2022). Pasal 5: nonphysical sexual acts aimed at the body, sexual desire, and/or reproductive organs "dengan maksud merendahkan harkat dan martabat seseorang berdasarkan seksualitas dan/atau kesusilaannya" are punished as pelecehan seksual nonfisik, with prison up to 9 months and/or a fine up to Rp10.000.000. Pasal 7: nonphysical harassment is a delik aduan (needs a complaint), except for victims who are children or persons with disabilities. The statute never says "catcalling"; a whistle or comment fits only if it meets these elements. No legal advice in the book.
- E4 (H abstract; M for frequency from the author's dissertation). Fairchild, K., & Rudman, L. A. (2008). Everyday stranger harassment and women's objectification. Social Justice Research, 21(3), 338-357. Stranger harassment is "a frequent experience for young adult women" and is "positively related to self-objectification" for women who coped passively, by self-blame, or benignly, "but not for women who used an uncommon, active coping strategy (e.g., confronting the harasser)". It was tied to fear of rape, and women who feared rape "were more likely to restrict their freedom of movement". Dissertation (Study 1, 228 US undergraduate women): 31% reported "catcalls, whistles, or stares" from strangers every few days or more.
- E5 (H, abstract). Collins, W. A. (2003). More than myth: The developmental significance of romantic relationships during adolescence. Journal of Research on Adolescence, 13(1), 1-24. Research was "impeded by erroneous assumptions that adolescent relationships are trivial and transitory". Five features: "involvement, partner selection, relationship content, quality, and cognitive and emotional processes".
- E6 (M, secondary review on PMC). Meier, A., & Allen, G. (2009). Romantic relationships from adolescence to young adulthood: Evidence from the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent Health. Citing Add Health data: "about one-quarter of 12-year-olds report romantic involvement, nearly 75 percent of all 18-year-olds report such involvement" (US data).
- E7 (H, full text). Casey, B. J., Jones, R. M., & Hare, T. A. (2008). The adolescent brain. Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences, 1124, 111-126. Adolescence brings "a heightened responsiveness to incentives and socioemotional contexts ... when impulse control is still relatively immature"; limbic systems appear developed by adolescence while control systems show "a protracted and linear developmental course into young adulthood"; "the prefrontal cortex is one of the last brain regions to mature". The paper also warns that "immature prefrontal function alone cannot account" for adolescent behavior; keep this caveat.
- E8 (M, abstract and figure caption). Steinberg, L. (2005). Cognitive and affective development in adolescence. Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 9(2), 69-74. Brain, behavioral and cognitive systems "mature along different timetables"; the figure caption calls the resulting gap a "speculated" period of "heightened vulnerability to problems in the regulation of affect and behavior".

## 6. Support, responsiveness, crab mentality, shared values (set F)

- F1 (H, abstract). Gable, S. L., Reis, H. T., Impett, E. A., & Asher, E. R. (2004). What do you do when things go right? The intrapersonal and interpersonal benefits of sharing positive events. JPSP, 87(2), 228-245. Telling others about positive events is linked to higher daily positive affect and well-being; "when others were perceived to respond actively and constructively (and not passively or destructively) to capitalization attempts, the benefits were further enhanced"; enthusiastic partners were linked to higher relationship well-being.
- F2 (H). Gable, S. L., & Reis, H. T. (2010). Good news! Capitalizing on positive events in an interpersonal context. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 42. Four response types: "active-constructive responses as enthusiastic and involved support, passive-constructive responses as quiet or silent support, active-destructive responses as reactions that undercut the event, and passive-destructive responses as reactions that ignore the event." Active-constructive responders show involvement and ask questions; passive-destructive responders change the subject or turn it to themselves.
- F3 (H abstract; M via secondary for the definition wording). Reis, H. T. (2012). Perceived partner responsiveness as an organizing theme for the study of relationships and well-being. In Campbell & Loving (Eds.), Interdisciplinary research on close relationships (pp. 27-52). APA. Responsiveness as "one possible unifying principle". Definition restated by Jolink et al. 2021 (PMC): feeling "understood, validated, and cared for" by a partner, measured with items like "My partner understands me" and "My partner sees the 'real' me."
- F4 (H, abstract). Drigotas, S. M., Rusbult, C. E., Wieselquist, J., & Whitton, S. W. (1999). Close partner as sculptor of the ideal self: Behavioral affirmation and the Michelangelo phenomenon. JPSP, 77(2), 293-323. The self "is shaped by a close partner's perceptions and behavior"; movement toward the ideal self is "a product of partner affirmation"; four studies found "strong associations between perceived partner affirmation and self movement toward the ideal self", and affirmation was associated with couple well-being and stability. No numbers.
- F5 (H for quotes; folk concept). Çavuş, B., & Sarpkaya, R. (2021). Measuring "crabs in a bucket" phenomenon at schools: A scale development study. Psycho-Educational Research Reviews, 10(2), 314-327. "a pattern of behavior based on the mentality of 'if I cannot have it, neither can you'"; people with crab mentality "aim to reduce the importance of those who are trying to be successful"; behaviors include "discouraging others, being jealous, undermining what they are doing". Crab mentality is mostly a folk metaphor; say so.
- F6 (H citation; M content). Feather, N. T. (1989). Attitudes towards the high achiever: The fall of the tall poppy. Australian Journal of Psychology, 41(3), 239-267. "Tall poppy syndrome" is the desire to cut down a person "elevated above their peers" (Springer encyclopedia entry). Use only the definition; no numbers.
- F7 (H, abstract). Montoya, R. M., Horton, R. S., & Kirchner, J. (2008). Is actual similarity necessary for attraction? A meta-analysis of actual and perceived similarity. Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, 25(6), 889-922. 460 effect sizes from 313 studies; attraction relates to actual similarity (r = .47) and perceived similarity (r = .39); in existing relationships the effect of actual similarity "was not significant" while perceived similarity still predicted attraction. Concerns attitude and personality similarity; the abstract does not name "values".
- F8 (H, abstract). Joel, S., Eastwick, P. W., et al. (2020). Machine learning uncovers the most robust self-report predictors of relationship quality across 43 longitudinal couples studies. PNAS, 117(32), 19061-19071. 43 datasets from 29 laboratories. Top relationship-specific predictors: "perceived-partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction, perceived-partner satisfaction, and conflict"; top individual differences: life satisfaction, negative affect, depression, attachment avoidance, attachment anxiety. Relationship-specific variables predicted up to 45% of variance at baseline; individual differences 21%. Change in quality "was largely unpredictable". Established couples, not partner choice; the abstract names no shared-values variable.
