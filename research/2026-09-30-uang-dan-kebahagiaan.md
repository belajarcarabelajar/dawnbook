# Deep Research Report: Uang dan Kebahagiaan (Money and Happiness)

**Date:** 2026-09-30 · **Status:** Draft v1 · **Consumer:** `docs/code-plan/plans/2026-09-30-dawnbook-pengaruh-uang-terhadap-kebahagiaan.md` (12-module Dawnbook book, author Iwan Kurniawan)

This report synthesizes the empirical economics and psychology literature on the relationship between money and happiness to ground the module architecture of a 12-chapter Indonesian mdBook on the Dawnbook platform. The investigation covers how subjective well-being is measured, the individual- and country-level evidence linking income to well-being, the plateau controversy and its 2023 resolution, the macro-level Easterlin debate, the psychological mechanisms (social comparison, hedonic adaptation, scarcity), and the spending-side evidence (experiential purchases, prosocial spending, buying time, materialism). The principal findings: income and well-being are robustly and positively associated, but the association is logarithmic, partly relative, and weakest for the chronically unhappy; how money is spent matters as much as how much is spent. The recommended action is the 12-module architecture in the Conclusion, ordered from measurement foundations through the income evidence to the controversies, mechanisms, and actionable spending science.

## 1. Measuring happiness: how the science reads the feeling

Before any money-happiness claim can be evaluated, the field needs a defensible definition of the dependent variable, and the consensus construct is **subjective well-being (SWB)**: people evaluating their own lives. Diener's canonical framework splits SWB into a cognitive component (life satisfaction, a global judgment of one's life) and affective components (the balance of frequent positive emotions such as joy and affection against negative emotions such as anger, worry, and sadness) [14]. Because the two components are empirically distinct, money can affect them differently, and several of the field's most famous controversies are actually disagreements about which component was being measured.

The workhorse instruments matter for interpreting the income studies. Global life evaluation is typically captured with the **Cantril Self-Anchoring Striving Scale** (the "ladder of life"), where respondents rate their current life from 0 (worst possible) to 10 (best possible); the World Happiness Report country rankings, in which Indonesia ranked 83rd of roughly 140+ countries with a score near 5.4 in the 2025 edition, are built on this single question fielded by the Gallup World Poll [15]. Experienced (emotional) well-being, by contrast, is measured by asking about feelings during specific recent episodes or moments, for example through the day-reconstruction method or smartphone experience sampling [1][2]. The distinction is not academic trivia: Kahneman and Deaton's headline finding is precisely that income buys the ladder (life evaluation) without buying the daily emotional experience [1].

Two measurement caveats shape honest reporting in a popular book. First, self-reports are sensitive to question wording, order, and the momentary mood of the respondent, so single-number country rankings should be presented as broad strokes rather than precise medals tables [14][15]. Second, when people evaluate "how happy" a hypothetical richer life would be, they suffer a **focusing illusion**: they overweight the aspects of life that money obviously changes (housing, leisure, status) and neglect the mundane ones that occupy most hours, which inflates the perceived happiness payoff of income [13].

## 2. The income-happiness association: robust, positive, logarithmic

The individual-level evidence is unambiguous in direction: richer people report higher well-being, on average, in nearly every dataset large enough to matter. Diener and Biswas-Diener's exhaustive review found consistent positive income-SWB correlations within countries, larger gaps in well-being between rich and poor nations, and the strongest effects at the low end of the income distribution [14]. Poverty is reliably associated with low life evaluation and elevated emotional distress, so the claim "money does not matter" is contradicted at the bottom of the distribution before any nuance begins [1][14].

The modern high-resolution picture comes from Matthew Killingsworth's experience-sampling study, which collected roughly 1.7 million real-time well-being reports from more than 33,000 employed United States adults through the Track Your Happiness smartphone application [2]. Well-being rose with income even far above the 75,000-dollar threshold, and the functional form was approximately **logarithmic**: each additional dollar buys less happiness than the previous one, but there is no visible ceiling within the sampled range [2]. The log form has an intuitive reading worth preserving in popular prose: proportional income increases (a 20 percent raise) correspond to similar happiness increments regardless of income level, which is why a small raise matters enormously at low income and negligibly at high income [2].

Cross-sectional and cohort evidence also shows why the simple average can mislead. The association holds on average, yet individual curves differ systematically: money appears to help most where it removes constraints, and least where unhappiness has non-financial roots, a heterogeneity that became the crux of the plateau dispute [1][2][3].

## 3. The plateau controversy and its 2023 resolution

The most cited data point in this literature is Kahneman and Deaton's analysis of about 450,000 daily Gallup-Healthways responses from United States residents, published in PNAS in 2010 [1]. Their two findings pull in different directions: life evaluation (the Cantril ladder) rose steadily with log income with no satiation, while **emotional well-being** (yesterday's enjoyment, happiness, stress, worry, sadness) improved with income only up to an annual threshold near 75,000 dollars (in 2010 purchasing power) and then flattened [1]. Their memorable conclusion, that "high income buys life satisfaction but not happiness," became the popular shorthand "money stops buying happiness at 75k."

Killingsworth's 2021 experience-sampling study directly contradicted the plateau: experienced well-being continued to rise with log income without flattening, in a sample an order of magnitude larger and measured moment-to-moment rather than by day-recall [2]. Rather than arguing past each other, the two camps ran an **adversarial collaboration** moderated by Barbara Mellers, combining both analytic approaches on the same data [3]. The resolution published in PNAS in 2023: for the happiest majority, experienced well-being keeps rising with income beyond 75,000 dollars and even beyond 100,000; but for an **unhappy minority** (roughly the least happy 15 to 20 percent of participants), the income-happiness curve flattens above about 100,000 dollars, consistent with the idea that money cannot repair unhappiness whose roots are heartbreak, bereavement, or clinical depression [3].

For a popular book, the resolved picture is both more accurate and more useful than either original headline: money buys happiness reliably at low income, marginally less per dollar as income grows, and not at all for some kinds of misery [1][2][3].

## 4. The macro view: the Easterlin paradox and the relativity of income

At the country level, Richard Easterlin observed in 1974 that within any country at a point in time richer people are happier, yet across time national happiness averages appeared not to rise with economic growth, the pattern later named the **Easterlin paradox** [4]. Easterlin's own explanation leaned on social comparison and habituation: as everyone's income rises together, relative standing is unchanged and aspirations rise with income, so average felt happiness stays put [4].

The paradox is contested, not settled. Stevenson and Wolfers' reassessment, using far richer international data than existed in 1974, found a statistically significant positive relationship between economic growth and average well-being across countries and over time, rejecting the strong form of the paradox [5]. Easterlin and colleagues have replied with re-analyses of long-run country series, and the debate continues in the literature [4][5]. For a popular book the honest framing is: the strong claim "growth buys nothing" is not supported, but the weak claim "growth buys less than people expect, and comparison eats part of the gain" survives the evidence [4][5][6].

The mechanism behind the weak claim is well documented at the individual level. Luttmer matched survey respondents to their census-neighborhood earnings and found that self-reported happiness falls as **neighbors' earnings** rise, holding one's own income constant; his estimates imply that a 10 percent rise in neighbors' earnings hurts well-being about as much as a comparable fall in one's own income [6]. Related experimental and survey work shows income **rank** (one's position in a comparison group) predicts satisfaction better than absolute income, and people often prefer a lower absolute income with higher rank over a higher absolute income with lower rank [6][7]. Income, in short, is partly a positional good, which explains why aggregate growth can disappoint individual expectations.

## 5. Psychological mechanisms: adaptation, comparison, and scarcity

**Hedonic adaptation** is the classic result. Brickman, Coates, and Janoff-Bulman's 1978 study compared major Illinois lottery winners (winds of 50,000 to 1,000,000 dollars) with controls and with paraplegic accident victims; lottery winners were not significantly happier than controls in the present, and took less pleasure in mundane events, while accident victims, though less happy than controls, rated their expected future happiness above neutral [7]. The study founded the "hedonic treadmill" metaphor: emotional responses to fortune shift, and the new baseline becomes ordinary. Later reviews qualify the strength of full adaptation (some life events leave permanent traces, and adaptation is incomplete for income in most data), so the modern framing is "adaptation is strong but not total" [7][14].

The **focusing illusion** explains why people systematically overestimate the happiness that money will bring: when asked to imagine a richer life, attention locks onto the changed features while the unchanged bulk of daily experience (commute, chores, relationships) disappears from the simulation [13]. Kahneman and colleagues showed that above-average-income respondents were more satisfied with their lives yet spent no more time in good moods, and devoted more hours to activities associated with negative affect such as work-related tension [13].

At the opposite pole, **scarcity** shows money's absence taxing the mind directly. Mani, Mullainathan, Shafir, and Zhao exploited the natural experiment of sugarcane farmers who are poor before harvest and rich after it: the same farmers performed substantially worse on cognitive tests before harvest, an effect the authors size as comparable to losing a full night's sleep or several IQ points [8]. Scarcity captures attention on immediate financial worries and leaves less bandwidth for everything else, a mechanism developed at length in Mullainathan and Shafir's *Scarcity* [8]. This is the cleanest evidence that money matters most exactly where income is lowest, and it grounds chapters on financial stress.

## 6. What people can do with money: experiences, others, time, and the materialist trap

The spending side of the literature is the most directly actionable. Van Boven and Gilovich established the core result: **experiential purchases** (trips, concerts, meals, classes) make people happier than equivalent **material purchases** (gadgets, clothes, objects), across recalled purchases, predicted purchases, and self/other reports [9]. The reasons recur across the literature: experiences resist unfavorable comparison, integrate into identity and memory, and are usually shared, while material goods invite side-by-side comparison and habituate quickly [9].

**Prosocial spending** is the smallest and most replicated intervention in the field. Dunn, Aknin, and Norton's 2008 Science paper showed across a survey, a field experiment, and a windfall experiment that spending money on others (buying gifts, donating) raised happiness while personal spending did not, even with as little as 5 dollars [10]. Follow-up work found the effect across cultures and, notably, among people spending on behalf of others in poor countries [10]. **Buying time** is the second lever: Whillans and colleagues showed across the United States, Canada, Denmark, and the Netherlands that people who use money to outsource disliked time-consuming tasks report greater life satisfaction, despite the persistent guilt around paying for such help [11].

The trap on the other side is **materialism**. Kasser and Ryan's foundational study found that people for whom financial success is a central life aspiration (relative to affiliation, community, and growth) show lower self-actualization, vitality, and adjustment, plus more anxiety and depression [12]. The negative association of materialistic values with well-being has been replicated across ages and cultures and meta-analytically; the proposed mechanisms are that extrinsic goals crowd out intrinsic ones and are psychologically insatiable, so chasing them cannot deliver sustained satisfaction [12].
